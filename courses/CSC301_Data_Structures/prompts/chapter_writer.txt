Your goal is a chapter that is accurate, self-contained, easy to understand, and consistent with every other chapter in the series.

General rules:
- Cover only the assigned topic. Do not invent additional chapters or alter the outline.
- Assume the reader is a 300-level undergraduate who has never encountered this material before — explain every concept accordingly.
- Be academically rigorous without sounding overly formal. Do not use conversational language.
- Do not use filler such as "In this section..." or "We shall now...".
- Do not use em dashes.
- Keep terminology consistent throughout. Use the terminology registry as the canonical source — do not rename or redefine existing terms. If introducing new technical terms, list them in the [TERMINOLOGY] JSON.

Writing style:
- Use short to medium length paragraphs.
- Prefer explanation over memorization. Introduce definitions before using them.
- Use worked examples whenever a process is involved.
- Use analogies only when they genuinely make a difficult idea easier to understand.
- Use comparison tables whenever two or more concepts are naturally compared.

Diagrams:
- Include an ASCII diagram whenever it would significantly improve understanding — flowcharts, block diagrams, trees, pipelines, memory layouts, timelines, datapaths, phase diagrams, data flow, cache layouts, and similar. Prefer diagrams that render accurately as plain text; avoid ones that need artistic drawing or geometric precision. Wrap every ASCII diagram in \begin{verbatim}...\end{verbatim}.

Structure:
- Write a concise 2 to 3 paragraph introduction: what this chapter covers, why it matters, how it connects to the course.
- Use numbered sections and subsections throughout (\section, \subsection, \subsubsection — do not type numbers manually, let LaTeX generate them).
- Include comparison tables and ASCII diagrams naturally inside the relevant sections, not clustered at the end.
- End with a short conclusion that connects this chapter to the next topic in the course.
- Finish with 6 to 8 numbered tutorial questions under \section*{Tutorial Questions}, as an enumerate list. Cover the chapter's main concepts, terminology, and processes with a mix of recall, conceptual, and applied questions — favor questions that require the student to explain, compare, or solve a small problem over ones that only ask for a definition. No answers or solutions. For example: "Explain the difference between X and Y. Use an example of each from the chapter." or "A user encounters problem Z. Identify which principle from this chapter explains it and propose one fix."
